That Girl Lola: The Show Where a Talent Unfolds One Big Audition at a Time

By Cara Leopardo, Founder & CEO · July 9, 2026

If you've ever started a show with your kids, gotten four minutes in, and then dove across the couch for the remote because somebody on screen said something you're now going to be explaining at dinner - this one's for you.

We have four kids. We've made that dive more times than we can count. It's the entire reason we built We The Studios in the first place: nobody was making TV for families anymore, so we did.

The shows below are the kind you can put on without a pre-screen, a hovering thumb on the remote, or that low-grade dread in your stomach. Real entertainment - funny, sweet, occasionally great - minus the profanity, the suggestive content, and the stuff that ages a kid five years in thirty seconds.

What "no bad language" should actually mean

Here's the problem with most "family-friendly" labels: they're a vibe, not a standard. A show gets called family-friendly because it has a kid in it, and then somewhere in episode three the dialogue turns and you're scrambling.

When we say a show is safe at We The Studios, we mean a real line in the sand: no profanity, no sexual content, and no glorifying alcohol. Not "mostly." Not "depends on the episode." Every series, every episode. That's the whole promise - you shouldn't have to keep auditing.

That Girl Lola

Lola is 11 years old. She's been dancing her whole life. She thinks of herself as a quadruple threat - dance, sing, model, and act.

This isn't another "inspirational talent show." It's the real deal: a kid who's homeschooled so she can pursue her dream, with a mom who owns a dance studio (and teaches her), a dad who runs the front desk, and an 8-year-old brother who ballrooms with her.

In one of the first episodes, Lola and her family are preparing for her big Chicago music video audition. The day starts at dawn - "super early to the city" they say. She's got makeup on (even though it's just a voiceover, she wants that first impression), nerves jangling, excitement bubbling.

She records two songs: "Don't Let Go" (already on Spotify and TikTok) and a new pop track she's calling I Cannot Breathe. She's working with someone the show calls "Georgy Porgy" - a vet who's worked with Michael Jackson and Mary J. Blige.

"He's writing appropriate music for her age. We would never dive any deeper than where an 11 year old is."

Kelly - Lola's Mom

That line tells you everything. There's no manipulation, no edgy themes, no forced nostalgia - just a gifted kid being guided by pros who get it.

And when she finally walks into the Chicago studio? It's not just about hitting the right notes. She's playing a daughter in a scene - with a fake mom and a fake brother - and she nails it. The director comes out, says her tape was "adorable," they absolutely loved it, and they'll keep her on file.

That's the magic: this isn't fantasy. It's preparation. Every day, Lola gets up, does schoolwork, heads to the studio, practices choreography, records vocals - all while being homeschooled so she can focus. Her mom even switches her from regular school to homeschool specifically because "with her auditions and where she wants to go as homeschool was a fit."

Why this is safe for everyone (even the eye-rolling tweens)

When we say a show is safe at We The Studios, we mean a real line in the sand: no profanity, no sexual content, and no glorifying alcohol. Not "mostly." Not "depends on the episode." Every series, every episode.

That Girl Lola passes with room to spare:

  • Language? Clean, energetic - occasional "super," "kinda," and "gotta," but zero curse words or inappropriate phrasing.
  • Themes? Homeschooling as a tool for ambition, family partnership (dad drives, mom coaches), mentorship (Georgy Porgy's studio is safe, structured, professional).
  • Tone? Uplifting, focused, joyful — no drama, no competition. The only "battle" is with practice routines and timing.

The tween who rolls their eyes at "babyish" shows won't walk away here. Lola doesn't talk down to her audience - she is them: smart, ambitious, trying to balance school and passion.

Clean doesn't mean dull. It just means you wrote the story without leaning on shock to keep people watching.

The moment that proves it

Midway through Season 1, Lola's mom (Kelly) sits with her before the big Chicago session and says:

"Lately, Lola, I've been having, like, doubts. In some days. It's just so hard."

Kelly - Lola's Mom

Then she follows up:

"And now things are falling into place because we believe this voiceover gig is a huge deal for Lola. It's her first national spot, and it definitely proves that hard work and energy pay off."

Kelly - Lola's Mom

That's the pivot: doubt → preparation → faith → success.

Not magic. Not luck. Work.

When Lola finally steps into the Chicago studio and hears herself on headphones? She gets "really excited." The headphones. The mic. The beat in the background. All the pieces coming together.

She doesn't just sing. She acts. She connects. And that's why they remember her: "her tape was so adorable."

What to expect in every episode

  • A home life that rotates between dance rehearsals and homeschool lessons
  • Parents who aren't stage moms/dads - they're coaches who listen
  • A studio culture that emphasizes respect, preparation, and fun
  • One kid's journey from audition to rehearsal to realization: "I want to make my second single different, like pop music"

You don't need to vet it. You don't need to hover. You can just... watch.

Because when you get home from work, the last thing you want is another show you have to manage.
